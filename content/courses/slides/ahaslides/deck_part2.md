## Presenter notes of the interactive slides

### B1 (core, about 1 min, after page 1)

**QR code: join**

Students join at ahaslides.com with code PWCXU or by scanning the QR code. Names are required on join, so the leaderboard and the spinner wheels show real names. Keep this slide up while the room settles and watch the count rise.

### B2 (core, about 1.5 min, after page 1)

**In one word: what can a satellite see about development?**

Warm-up, no right answer. Do not correct anyone yet. The same prompt returns at the end of class, so take a screenshot of this cloud for the comparison.

### B3 (core, about 1 min, after page 1)

**Before we start: how confident are you?**

Baseline confidence on a 1 to 5 scale. The same three statements return before the exit ticket, so the class can see its own change. Read the averages aloud without comment.

### B4 (core, about 0.5 min, after page 1)

**Questions? Ask them here at any time during class**

Q&A stays open on every slide (presentation setting: Q&A on all slides). Questions can be anonymous. Upvotes push a question to the top. Check the Q&A at the leaderboard break and at the end.

### B5 (core, about 1 min, after page 30)

**Have you ever worked with satellite data?**

A quick baseline. If most answer A, slow down at the night lights apps; if many answer C, ask one of them to help a neighbor during the hands-on stops.

### B6 (core, about 1.5 min, after page 31)

**In one word: why are official statistics weak in many poor countries?**

Collect words for a minute. The next slide gives the answer in three words: data are unavailable, unreliable and uncomparable. Check how many of the three words the cloud already holds.

### B7 (opt, about 1.5 min, after page 32)

**Cold call: give an example of missing or unreliable data from your country**

Spin; the wheel holds the joined names. Typical examples: an old census, GDP rebased by a large amount, no data below the state level, or a conflict area with no surveys. Poor Numbers (Jerven, 2013) on the slide tells this story for Africa.

### B8 (core, about 1 min, after page 33)

**Complete the case for satellite data**

Answer: reliable, available and comparable, the mirror image of the previous slide. The papers on the slide, many of them highly cited, use these data to measure growth, convergence and inequality across regions.

### B9 (core, about 1.5 min, after page 35)

**Drop a pin on your home region**

The world at night as a pin board. The heatmap shows where the class comes from. Ask two students whether their home region is brighter or darker than they expected, and why.

### B10 (opt, about 1.5 min, after page 35)

**Cold call: name a bright place and a dark place on this map. Why the difference?**

Expected answers: income, population density, electrification, and also deserts, forests and ice with nobody living there. The next video makes the same point: dark is not always poor.

### B11 (core, about 1 min, after page 37)

**Night lights measure income directly.**

Answer: False. Night lights are a proxy: they track economic activity, population and electricity use together. They work well where official data are weak, but they need calibration against data on income or output, as the World Bank video shows for South Asia.

### B12 (core, about 8 min, after page 39)

**Lights app stop: share a screenshot of the most striking change, with the place in the caption**

The first hands-on stop, about eight minutes. Students open https://carlosmendez777.users.earthengine.app/view/worldviirs-like on their laptops, move the slider between the two years, and upload a screenshot with the place and their explanation in the caption. Show the wall at the end and ask two students to explain theirs.

### B13 (core, about 1.5 min, after page 40)

**Drop a pin on North Korea**

No labels on the map. Most pins should land in the dark area north of the bright South Korea. The border is a light border: same geography, very different economies. Ask what else, besides income, could make a country this dark.

### B14 (core, about 1 min, after page 40)

**The next map mixes three years as colors: red = 2013, green = 2003, blue = 1993. A place lit in all three years looks...**

Answer: B, white. Red, green and blue at full brightness add up to white, so stable old lights are white. The next quiz asks about new lights.

### B15 (core, about 1 min, after page 40)

**Same map. A place dark in 1993 and 2003 but lit in 2013 looks...**

Answer: C, red, because red is 2013 only. Yellow (red plus green) means lit since 2003. On the next slide, much of China is red and yellow, Japan is white, and North Korea stays dark.

### B16 (core, about 6 min, after page 41)

**RGB app stop: name a city or region that lit up recently (red or yellow) and its country.**

The second hands-on stop, about six minutes. Students open https://jhowarth.users.earthengine.app/view/rgb-nighttime-lights and look for red or yellow places. Good finds: inland China, parts of India, the Gulf, West Africa. Ask what changed there between 1993 and 2013.

### B17 (core, about 1 min, after page 42)

**Which sensor gives sharper night lights, with less blurring and no top-coding in city centers?**

Answer: B, VIIRS. DMSP blurs light into nearby areas and caps bright city centers, so it understates inequality and growth in dense places. Gibson argues for VIIRS, and for VIIRS-like series that extend it back in time. Landsat is a daytime sensor.

### B19 (opt, about 1 min, after page 43)

**Night lights work equally well in dense cities and in sparsely populated rural areas.**

Answer: False. In sparse rural areas, much economic activity (farming) emits little light, so lights understate rural output. The Waikato video makes this point with the United States.

### B20 (opt, about 1 min, after page 44)

**Which recommended book will you open first?**

### B21 (core, about 2.5 min, after page 45)

**What can daytime images show that night lights cannot?**

Two minutes of ideas, then voting. Expected: roof materials, building size, roads, crops, water and green space, all visible in rural areas where night lights are dark. Machine learning turns these features into poverty estimates (next slides).

### B22 (core, about 1.5 min, after page 46)

**Which images fit each task best?**

Answer: nighttime images for city brightness and electrification; daytime images for roofs and crops. The slide summary pairs nighttime images with urban development and daytime images with rural development.

### B23 (core, about 1 min, after page 46)

**Should a government use satellite images to decide who receives cash transfers?**

Opinion poll before the BBC video on Togo, where satellite images and phone data targeted cash during the pandemic. Do not discuss yet; the 2x2 after the video does.

### B24 (opt, about 2 min, after page 47)

**Targeting cash with satellite data: where does each risk land?**

Unscored. Compare with the poll before the video: did the video change minds? Missed poor households in rich-looking areas is the classic error of area-based targeting.
