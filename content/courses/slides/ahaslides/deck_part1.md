## Presenter notes of the interactive slides

### A1 (core, about 1 min, after page 1)

**QR code: join**

Students join at ahaslides.com with code X0CIY or by scanning the QR code. Names are required on join, so the leaderboard and the spinner wheels show real names. Keep this slide up while the room settles and watch the count rise.

### A2 (core, about 1.5 min, after page 1)

**In one word: what makes a region developed?**

Warm-up, no right answer. Do not correct anyone yet. The same prompt returns at the end of class, so take a screenshot of this cloud for the comparison.

### A3 (core, about 1 min, after page 1)

**Before we start: how confident are you?**

Baseline confidence on a 1 to 5 scale. The same three statements return before the exit ticket, so the class can see its own change. Read the averages aloud without comment.

### A4 (core, about 0.5 min, after page 1)

**Questions? Ask them here at any time during class**

Q&A stays open on every slide (presentation setting: Q&A on all slides). Questions can be anonymous. Upvotes push a question to the top. Check the Q&A at the leaderboard break and at the end.

### A5 (opt, about 1.5 min, after page 2)

**After the video: in your country, which problem would better maps help most?**

No right answer. The Esri video shows maps answering questions in a crisis: where, how many, who is affected. Ask one or two students why they chose their answer, then move to the outline.

### A6 (core, about 2 min, after page 4)

**Why study regional development? Rank your top three reasons**

Each student ranks three of five reasons; the result uses a Borda count. Use the top reason as the thread of the lecture: every section that follows returns to it. Section 1 answers why regions matter, section 2 how to measure them from space, and section 3 which tools to use.

### A7 (core, about 1.5 min, after page 5)

**Drop a pin on the poorest part of this photo**

The Rio de Janeiro photo, now as a pin board. Most pins should land on the informal settlement in the foreground, a few on the high-rise blocks. Ask the room what visual cues they used: roofs, density, roads, green space. Those cues are exactly what daytime satellite images measure in section 2.

### A8 (core, about 2 min, after page 5)

**How would you measure the development gap in this photo? Name one indicator**

The slide asked how we can measure, predict and explain these gaps. Collect indicators for 90 seconds, then open voting (two votes each) and read the top three. Point out which ones need a survey and which ones can be seen from above.

### A9 (core, about 1 min, after page 6)

**This Sao Paulo photo was taken in 2004. By 2021, the gap between the two neighborhoods was...**

A prediction poll; do not reveal the answer. The next slide shows the same spot in 2004 and 2021: the favela of Paraisopolis and the luxury towers next to it look almost unchanged, and El Pais (2022) reported that little has changed in nearly 20 years. The answer is B, about the same: gaps can persist for decades.

### A10 (core, about 3 min, after page 7)

**Share a photo of a development gap you have seen in your home region**

Students upload one photo from their phone, with a caption that names the place. Photos from the internet are fine if they have none of their own. Pick two photos and ask their owners what the gap is and whether it shows a geographic pattern.

### A11 (opt, about 1.5 min, after page 9)

**Cold call: what can the view from above show that a GDP table cannot?**

Spin the wheel; it fills itself with joined names. Good answers: where growth happens inside a city or country, how fast cities expand, change over time without waiting for a census, and places with no official statistics at all.

### A12 (opt, about 1.5 min, after page 10)

**Tap each goal: why does it need local data?**

Students tap the numbered pins on their phones and read at their own pace while you talk through the slide. No scoring. Ask which goal matters most in their own country, which leads into the next ranking.

### A13 (opt, about 1.5 min, after page 10)

**Which goal most needs data for small regions? Rank your top three**

No right answer. Many students put SDG 1 first: poverty maps are the classic use of subnational data. Note the goal that ranks last and ask one student to argue for it.

### A14 (core, about 1 min, after page 11)

**Do we have enough data to study regional development in your country?**

An honest answer from the room before the data tour. Students from countries with recent censuses tend to answer A or B. Keep the result in mind: section 2 argues that satellites fill exactly the gap behind answers B and C.

### A15 (core, about 1 min, after page 11)

**Next video: Rosling starts in 1810. In that year, life expectancy in every country was below...**

Answer: A, 40 years. In 1810 all countries were poor and sick, with life expectancy below 40. The four-minute video that follows shows 200 years of divergence and then convergence. Play it in full (core).

### A16 (core, about 1 min, after page 12)

**Now Rosling splits China into provinces. Rich Shanghai matches which country in health and wealth?**

Answer: B, Italy. The next video starts at 3:40, where Rosling splits China: Shanghai has the health and wealth of Italy, the inland province Guizhou is like Pakistan, and the poorest rural west is like Ghana. One country holds the whole world inside it.

### A17 (core, about 1 min, after page 13)

**Match each part of China to the country it resembles (Rosling, 2009)**

Answer: Shanghai is Italy, Guizhou is Pakistan, the rural west is Ghana. The point for the course: a national average for China describes none of these places. That is the case for subnational data.

### A18 (core, about 1 min, after page 13)

**The McKinsey study Pixels of Progress splits the world into how many micro regions?**

Answer: C. Pixels of Progress (McKinsey Global Institute) uses more than 40,000 micro regions, about 230 times more granular than a country view; each averages about 180,000 people. Night lights helped build it. The next two slides show one bubble for the world and then the world through a regional microscope.

### A19 (opt, about 1 min, after page 14)

**A world average of 73 years of life and USD 16,800 of income per person describes most places well.**

Answer: False. The single bubble on the previous slide hides a wide cloud of micro regions, far above and below the average in both life expectancy and income, as the next slide shows. Averages are a summary, not a description.

### A20 (core, about 1 min, after page 17)

**Complete the definition of a raster image**

Answer: pixels, value. A raster is a set of pixels and also an array of numbers, which is why satellite images can enter a regression like any other data. Polygons and points are vector data, the other main type of spatial data.

### A21 (core, about 0.5 min, after page 17)

**Leaderboard**

Halfway leaderboard. Then the GeoQuery video and the data tour.

### A22 (core, about 1 min, after page 18)

**GeoQuery requires coding or mapping software.**

Answer: False. GeoQuery (AidData) filters and aggregates geospatial data to provinces or districts and returns a spreadsheet, without code or mapping software. That makes it the easiest entry point for the portal stop that follows the tour.

### A23 (core, about 8 min, after page 26)

**Portal stop: find one subnational indicator for your country. Write the indicator, the portal and the level (state, district or pixel).**

The hands-on stop, about eight minutes on laptops. Portals: http://geo.aiddata.org, https://globaldatalab.org, https://sedac.ciesin.columbia.edu, https://pipmaps.worldbank.org/en/data/datatopics/poverty-portal/home, https://developers.google.com/earth-engine/datasets, https://gee-community-catalog.org. Suggest GeoQuery or Global Data Lab for beginners. Walk around and help. Close by reading three answers that use different levels, and ask which level would be best for their own research question.

### A24 (core, about 1.5 min, after page 26)

**Match each portal to what it offers**

Answer as listed. Earth Engine and the community catalog are the raster archives behind many of these products; they return in Part 3 with Earth Engine itself.

### A25 (opt, about 2 min, after page 27)

**Split 100 points of research time across these data sources**

No right answer. Compare the average split with the room answers to the data poll earlier: rooms that said data are scarce often give satellites more points. The next video argues for geospatial big data.

### AR1 (core, about 0.75 min, after page 75)

**In the Rosling video, which Chinese province looks like Pakistan?**

Rapid-fire review: four questions, 20 seconds each, speed bonus on. Answer: B, Guizhou, the poor inland province.

### AR2 (core, about 0.75 min, after page 75)

**A raster image is best described as...**

Answer: A. Borders are vector data; survey tables are not spatial at all.

### AR3 (core, about 0.75 min, after page 75)

**The Sao Paulo photos from 2004 and 2021 show that the gap...**

Answer: C. Regional gaps can persist for decades.

### AR4 (core, about 0.75 min, after page 75)

**Which portal offers a subnational Human Development Index?**

Answer: B, Global Data Lab.

### A91 (opt, about 1.5 min, after page 75)

**Again, in one word: what makes a region developed?**

The opening prompt again. Show the screenshot of the first cloud next to this one and ask what changed.

### A92 (core, about 1 min, after page 75)

**After class: how confident are you now?**

The same three statements as the opening scale. Compare the averages with the baseline aloud. The statement with the smallest gain is the one to revisit next class.

### A93 (core, about 2 min, after page 75)

**Exit ticket: what is still unclear after today? One sentence.**

The exit ticket. Responses carry names, so they can be followed up. Skim them after class and open the next session with the two most common points.

### A94 (core, about 1 min, after page 75)

**Duck race raffle**

Prize raffle. Every joined student becomes a duck and the winner is pure luck, so students who scored low on the quizzes still have a chance.
