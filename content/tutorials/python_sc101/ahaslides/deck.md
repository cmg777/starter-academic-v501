## 9. Content

- **Title:** The lab: 39 states, 31 years, one treated state, and one estimand
- **Image page:** 8 of 36

**Notes:** The data are the panel of Abadie, Diamond, and Hainmueller (2010), the same file as the Stata edition. The panel is balanced, with 31 rows for each state. The original authors had already removed states with large tobacco programs or large tax increases, such as Arizona, Massachusetts, and Oregon. The four covariates are log GDP per capita, the share aged 15–24, the retail price of cigarettes, and beer consumption. Beer data start only in 1984, so the beer average over 1980–1988 rests on 1984–1988 alone. The method estimates what Proposition 99 did in California, not what it would do in Nevada or Texas.

## 10. Content

- **Title:** A synthetic control is a weighted recipe of donor states
- **Image page:** 9 of 36

**Notes:** Equation 1 chooses nonnegative donor weights that sum to one. These weights make synthetic California resemble California on each of the seven predictors before 1989. Because the weights form a convex combination, synthetic California can sell neither more than the highest donor nor less than the lowest donor. In 1988, California sold 90.1 packs, inside the donor range from 55.0 packs in Utah to 180.4 packs in New Hampshire. A convex combination can therefore reach it.

The predictor weights are not fixed in advance. An outer search chooses them so that the synthetic path also reproduces sales in California from 1970 to 1988. Many different predictor weights can lead to the same donor weights, a point that returns on a later slide.

---

## 11. Content

- **Title:** The effect is the gap between California and its synthetic twin
- **Image page:** 10 of 36

**Notes:** Equation 2 applies the optimal weights to the sales of the donors in each year. The gap then subtracts this synthetic path from actual sales in California. Equation 3 averages the 12 yearly gaps from 1989 to 2000 into one number, the ATT. In mlsynth, these objects are `res.counterfactual`, `res.gap`, and `res.att`. Exercise 2 of the post rebuilds the synthetic path by hand and matches `res.counterfactual` up to floating-point error.

---

## 12. Content

- **Title:** One configuration dictionary drives every mlsynth estimator
- **Image page:** 11 of 36

**Notes:** The mlsynth library organizes every estimator around one pattern: a dictionary of settings, an estimator class, and a call to `fit()`. Five keys describe the data, two keys describe the predictors, and the remaining keys choose the algorithm. The backend `mscmt` runs the nested search over the predictor weights V and the donor weights W, and the seed fixes its global search. The fit takes about one second, and the key `inference` stays off until the placebo test, because it would add 38 placebo fits.

The library validates the dictionary with pydantic before it computes anything. A misspelled key such as `covariate_window` therefore raises an `MlsynthConfigError` instead of failing silently. One trap remains in version 1.0.0: if every predictor spans the same years, the library first drops every year in which any predictor is missing. The lagged sales therefore receive their own one-year windows.

## 25. Content

- **Title:** California has the largest MSPE ratio of all 39 states, so $p = 1/39 = 0.026$
- **Image page:** 21 of 36

**Notes:** The answer to the vote: C. Georgia, Virginia, and Missouri rank second to fourth, and each fits better than California before 1989. Their pre-treatment MSPEs are 1.41, 2.74, and 1.09, against 3.08 for California. The ratio rewards a close fit before 1989 and a large gap afterward, so a badly fitted state has a large denominator and a small ratio. The p-value is the share of the 39 states whose ratio is at least as large as that of California. California always counts itself, so rank 1 of 39 gives p = 1/39 = 0.026.

The built-in test of mlsynth reaches the same verdict. It ranks the square root of the ratio, 11.36 for California, and reports rank 1 of 39 with p = 0.026. Its placebo fits leave California out of every donor pool, while our loop keeps California in, as synth2 does.

## 26. Content

- **Title:** The cut(2) filter keeps 20 states, and California still ranks first: $p = 0.050$
- **Image page:** 22 of 36

**Notes:** The option cut(2) of synth2 drops every placebo whose pre-treatment MSPE exceeds twice that of California, which is 3.08. It removes 19 states, including Utah and New Hampshire, whose extreme sales no weighted average can reach. It also removes Rhode Island, whose pre-treatment MSPE is about 20 times that of California. California still ranks first among the 20 states that remain, so p = 1/20 = 0.050. After 1989, its gap lies below every retained placebo in 9 of the 12 years and ends at −25.73 packs, against −18.77 for Georgia.

The cutoff is a convention, and one state sits close to it. South Dakota is removed at 2.026 times the pre-treatment MSPE of California, while Illinois is kept at 1.867. Another seed or software stack could therefore keep 21 states and change the p-value to 1/21.

## 27. Content

- **Image page:** 23 of 36

**Notes:** The pointwise test repeats the ranking in each year, using the gaps instead of the ratio. Proposition 99 was designed to lower sales, so the left-sided p-value is the relevant one. ## 32. Content

- **Title:** Python reproduces the Stata benchmark at every step that matters
- **Image page:** 27 of 36

**Notes:** The scorecard compares each step with the Stata log. The donor weights differ by less than 0.002, because the two optimizers stop at slightly different points of a flat objective. Stata rounds its weights to three decimals before it predicts, and those rounded weights reproduce its ATT of −19.0018 by hand. The placebo rank, the p-value, and the 20 states kept by cut(2) agree exactly. The predictor weights differ sharply, but V is not identified, so this difference needs no reconciliation.

Two further differences have simple sources. The R² differs because synth2 divides by the variation of synthetic California, and with that definition Python also gives 0.974. The MSPE ratio of California is 123.5 in Stata against 129.0 in Python, because the Stata placebo command refits California with a weaker search.

## 33. Content

- **Title:** Four mlsynth estimators, one sign: the ATT ranges from −15.61 to −21.39
- **Image page:** 28 of 36

**Notes:** The tour reuses the same dictionary with three other estimators. The outcome-only fit matches the 19 pre-treatment outcomes directly and gives −19.51 with six donors. ## 34. Content

- **Title:** The gap was already large before Proposition 10 raised the tax again in 1999
- **Image page:** 29 of 36

**Notes:** We steelman the objection first, because the largest gaps arrive in 1999 and 2000. Proposition 10 raised the state cigarette tax by a further 50 cents per pack in January 1999. ## 37. Content

- **Title:** "$p = 0.026$ is the probability of no effect": it is a rank, not a probability
- **Image page:** 32 of 36

**Notes:** This misreading is common in applied work. Suppose that the program had no effect in any state and that every state were equally likely to be the treated one. Each state would then be equally likely to have the largest ratio. California would then hold the top rank with probability 1/39, which is exactly the p-value. The p-value is thus a statement about the data under the null, not about the truth of the null.

## 39. Content

- **Title:** "The smallest pre-treatment error wins": a close fit is necessary, not sufficient

**Notes:** Fit before 1989 is a necessary condition, not a ranking device. Negative weights let a counterfactual leave the range of the donors, which the simplex constraint of the baseline forbids. The weaker placebo test of the outcome-only fit shows that a closer fit need not yield stronger evidence.

- **Title:** "cut(2) can only strengthen the evidence": it trades resolution for comparability
- **Image page:** 35 of 36

**Notes:** A badly fitted state has a large pre-treatment MSPE, so its ratio tends to be small already, and the filter seldom helps California climb the ranking. What the filter buys is comparability of the gaps themselves, which matters for the gap figure and the pointwise test. What it costs is resolution, because every p-value then moves on a coarser grid.
