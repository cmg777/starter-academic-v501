# Data dictionary - Brexit and the OECD: Quarterly GDP Panel

On 23 June 2016 the United Kingdom voted to leave the European Union. To estimate what the vote cost the British economy, the tutorial builds a "synthetic" United Kingdom: a weighted blend of other rich economies that tracks UK output quarter by quarter before the referendum. The gap between the real and the synthetic UK after the vote is the estimated effect. This page documents the two files behind that exercise. <code>brexit_analysis</code> is the estimation sample: quarterly real GDP and six economic covariates for 24 OECD economies from 1995Q1 to 2020Q4. The United Kingdom is the single treated unit and the other 23 countries are the donor pool. Every estimator in the post (difference-in-differences, synthetic control, demeaned synthetic control, synthetic difference-in-differences, MASC and augmented synthetic control) uses this file and only its outcome <code>log_rgdp</code> in the headline specification. <code>brexit_panel_wide</code> is the full source panel it was cut from: all 36 OECD countries from 1960Q1 to 2020Q4 and twelve underlying national-accounts and labour series, so that you can rebuild the donor pool, the window or the covariates yourself.


Companion data for the post <https://carlos-mendez.org/tutorials/python_sc_dsc_sdid/>. Each dataset ships as a labeled Stata `.dta` (v118) plus its source file. Generated by `build_data_dictionary.py`.

## Known limitations & caveats

- <strong>The last nine quarters are OECD projections, not data.</strong> The panel is the November 2018 vintage of the OECD Economic Outlook, so it contains observed values only through 2018Q3. From 2018Q4 to 2020Q4 every series is the OECD's projection. The data show it plainly: the average absolute quarter-to-quarter change in GDP growth falls from 0.77 points (2010–2018Q2) to 0.06 points (2019–2020), and 2020 shows no COVID-19 collapse. Both evaluation quarters of the post (2018Q4 and 2019Q4) are therefore projected, so the estimated 'shortfall' compares projected UK output with projected donor output. This inference is drawn from the data; the source file does not flag projected values.
- <strong><code>log_rgdp</code> is not the log of GDP in currency units.</strong> It is the log of GDP indexed to each country's 1995 average, so values start near 0 in 1995 and measure growth since then. Comparing levels across countries is meaningless; comparing paths is the point.
- <strong><code>treated</code> hard-codes one treatment date.</strong> It switches on at 2016Q3. The 2016Q2 robustness check and every evaluation window in the post build their own indicator instead (<code>treat</code> in <code>window()</code>).
- <strong>Trade shares above 1 are real, not errors.</strong> Luxembourg (exports up to 2.18 times GDP), Ireland and Hungary re-export much of what they import, so exports and imports can exceed GDP. Ireland's labour productivity growth of 19.6% in 2015Q1 reflects the relocation of multinational assets, not a real productivity boom.
- <strong>The donor pool is itself a choice.</strong> Twelve of the 36 countries are dropped because at least one stored series is incomplete: Chile, Czech Republic, Denmark, Estonia, Greece, Israel, Latvia, Lithuania, Mexico, Poland, Slovenia and Turkey. Czech Republic, Estonia and Israel are dropped only for a gap in an inflation series that no specification uses.
- <strong>The paper's text and its code differ.</strong> de Brabander et al. (2025) write that all variables are standardised and log-transformed; their replication code (and these files) use log(<code>real_gdp</code>) for the outcome and untransformed shares, growth rates and ratios for the covariates.
- <strong>Units of the raw series are not documented.</strong> The <code>_raw</code> volume series are in national currency (or persons), at the prices and base year of the OECD Economic Outlook; the source file records neither the base year nor whether series are seasonally adjusted. Magnitudes are consistent with annualised quarterly levels (UK <code>real_gdp_raw</code> in 2016Q2 is 1.96 trillion pounds).
- <strong>Coverage before 1995 is uneven.</strong> In the wide panel, GDP and its components exist for 30 of the 36 countries, employment for 32, labour productivity for 28, unemployment for 31 and the working-age population for all 36. Several series start after 1960 (Lithuania's labour series in 2002Q1). Empty cells mean the source has no value.
